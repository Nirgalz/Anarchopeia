## Problem

Defining domination solely by observed abuse overlooks credible but unexercised arbitrary powers, structural dependencies, and institutional arrangements that make people vulnerable even when no particular actor intends to dominate.

## Objectives

Identify domination across interpersonal, institutional and systemic settings while distinguishing it from all asymmetries, legitimate constraints and material limits.

## Proposal

### Working definition

> **Domination** is a relationship or systemic configuration in which an individual or group is exposed to arbitrary power—whether exercised or credibly exercisable—arising directly or indirectly from individuals, groups, organizations, institutions, or systemic mechanisms, **without effective means to control, contest, constrain, or transform that power**.

The **real possibility** of arbitrary interference matters even where no abuse has yet occurred, or no intention to dominate can be established. A purely abstract ability to harm someone is insufficient.

Three overlapping forms merit investigation:

- **Interpersonal:** a person has power over another without effective accountability or contestation.
- **Institutional:** an organization's rules allow insufficiently controlled or challengeable power.
- **Systemic:** multiple economic, political or organizational mechanisms together produce dependencies or power asymmetries without one identifiable central decision-maker.

**Not every power asymmetry is necessarily domination.** Specialized authority, shared rules, and collective decisions can involve constraint without being arbitrary. Material scarcity is a constraint, but rules governing access to a scarce resource may establish domination.

**Effective control and contestation** require more than rights on paper. Mechanisms must be practically accessible, independent enough to address contested power, capable of meaningful remedies, and usable without retaliation. This is a set of investigative questions, not yet a complete theory of legitimacy.

This conception includes the **potential** for arbitrary power; investigating only actual abuses would miss structural vulnerabilities.

Related proposals: [Project aims](../0001-aims/en.md) and [Emancipation](../0002-emancipation/en.md).

## Alternatives

- Identify domination only when an abuse actually occurs.
- Treat every unequal distribution of power as domination.
- Require the conscious intention of an identifiable actor.
- Restrict domination to formal institutions while excluding systemic relations.

These definitions should be tested against counterexamples.

## Criticism and limitations

- The term **arbitrary** needs a clearer criterion: legality, consent and majority approval may not be sufficient.
- Contestability cannot necessarily remove every dependence or power imbalance.
- A theoretical capacity to interfere must be distinguished from a credible power in a real relationship.
- Attribution of systemic domination may be difficult when no single agent directs the outcome.
- Procedures claiming to prevent domination may themselves create bureaucratic gatekeepers.

## Evaluation

Potential investigation questions:

- Who can alter whose choices, options or circumstances without accountability?
- Could arbitrary interference occur even when it has not yet happened?
- Are complaint, appeal, public scrutiny and revision procedures materially usable?
- Can the subjects of an institution influence the rules structuring their dependence?
- What protections exist against retaliation and against informal concentrations of influence?
- Are there institutional alternatives that reduce this exposure without obstructing necessary collective coordination?

## Proposed Manifesto Entry

**Domination** exists where an individual or group is exposed, directly or indirectly, to **arbitrary power**, exercised or credibly exercisable, without effective means to control, contest, constrain or transform that power. Domination may be interpersonal, institutional or systemic; it does not require an actual abuse or a conscious intention to dominate.

Not every asymmetry of authority, legitimate collective constraint or material limitation constitutes domination. The issue is real exposure to arbitrary power, not a merely abstract possibility of harm.

Rights of challenge and review must be effective in practice: accessible, sufficiently independent, capable of meaningful remedies and exercisable without retaliation.

## Research

Research to develop within this proposal:

- Compare freedom as non-domination with non-interference and broader critical-theory accounts of domination.
- Investigate institutional and systemic power that cannot be reduced to an individual's intention.
- Develop criteria for arbitrariness and effective contestation, including access, independent review and remedies.
- Examine cases of dependency in employment, housing, education, public services, and emergency authority.
- Look for counterexamples in which power is asymmetric but non-arbitrary, or formal safeguards fail in practice.

### Historical notes to investigate — not adopted

The [older founding principles](../../txt/principes%20fondateurs.txt) restrict specialized or emergency authority to expertise and limited scope. Compare this with the current definition: **expertise alone does not legitimate arbitrary authority**. The [moral essay](../../txt/morale.txt) sometimes rejects *every* authority, conflicting with the more precise distinction between legitimate coordination and domination. Investigate the tension rather than adopting either absolute claim; also consider informal prestige and personality cults.
