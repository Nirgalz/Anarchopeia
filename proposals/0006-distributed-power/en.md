## Problem

The initiating article's call for the “atomization” of power is insufficiently defined: **formal distribution** of responsibilities may coexist with concentrated informal influence, unaccountable local authorities or blurred accountability. Concentration can enable arbitrary power, but maximal fragmentation may also impair coordination, access to services and opportunities for collective action. Oversight bodies can themselves become sources of domination. Distribution, exercise and oversight must therefore be assessed against the **two distinct aims** in AP-0001, without equating influence, expertise or asymmetry with domination under AP-0003.

## Objectives

- Examine distribution of power as a **potential means**, not an inherent end, by assessing effects on emancipation and non-domination.
- Keep **all power**, including oversight power, justifiable, limited and effectively controllable; scrutinize both decisions and the rules that authorize them.
- Identify **formal and informal power** without automatically classifying differences in expertise, resources or influence as domination.
- Consider **delegation** as a possible arrangement for collective action, subject to effective control and revision; recall is one mechanism, neither universal nor sufficient by itself.
- Compare concrete institutional arrangements, coordination costs, capture risks and uneven effects on people affected.

## Proposal

### General principle: distribution is instrumental

> **Distribution and oversight of power** aim to organize the allocation, exercise and coordination of decision-making authority in ways that support emancipation and prevent domination. Distribution is **not an end in itself**. Concentration and fragmentation must both be assessed according to justification, effective limits, control and their consequences for individuals and collectives.

The historical language of “atomization” is **neither a commitment to maximal fragmentation nor an adopted institutional rule**. A specialized authority or centralized coordinating body may be justified and effectively controlled; multiple formally autonomous authorities may, conversely, perpetuate domination. More decision-making centers do not mechanically reduce arbitrariness.

### Allocation, distribution, exercise and control

Distinguish **allocation** of competences (who may decide what), their **distribution** (across actors and levels), their **actual exercise** (how decisions are prepared and implemented) and their **oversight** (who can demand accounts, challenge and change decisions or their governing rules).

Oversight has **two complementary objects**:
1. **Exercise of authority:** compliance with rules, reasons for decisions, appeals, correction and remedy where appropriate.
2. **Constitutive rules of authority:** the ability to question, limit and revise competences, mandates and the procedures for changing them.

Under [AP-0003 — Domination](../0003-domination/en.md), consider **justification, effective limitation and meaningful oversight together**, in context. Merely formal safeguards or majority votes cannot independently exclude arbitrariness. **Oversight actors are themselves subject to the same requirements.** There is no need to posit an infinite chain of superior overseers: combine, where relevant, mutual checks, accessible remedies, verifiable information, direct or indirect participation of people affected and procedures to revise rules. Specify who is responsible, and how decisions and the oversight structure itself can be corrected.

### Formal and informal power

Examine effective means of steering decisions beyond formal office: agenda-setting, selection or withholding of information, specialized expertise that is hard to challenge, control of funds and resources, personal relationships and influence networks, implementation capacity and access to remedies.

**Influence, superior expertise or unequal resources do not by themselves prove domination.** Identify the specific mechanisms through which asymmetry could expose people to **credible arbitrary power**, and determine whether real opportunities for justification, limitation and challenge exist. Remedies against informal concentration must also remain controllable and must not erase expertise, autonomy or plural expression.

A group without formal hierarchy may still develop informal elites; an organization with explicit offices can make responsibilities more visible and contestable. Neither arrangement is automatically emancipatory.

### Delegation, recall and continuity

**Allocating** a competence, **delegating** its exercise, and **recalling** or changing a delegation are distinct operations. Delegation can support expertise, continuity, coordination and collective action, but it can also create information asymmetries and dependencies.

Study, without prescribing universal procedures: mandate **scope**, duration and review periods, obligations to give reasons and disclose relevant information, conflicts of interest, meaningful routes to challenge decisions, amendment or withdrawal of mandates, safeguards for affected people, and continuity of essential functions.

**Recall may contribute to oversight but does not guarantee it.** It must be practically available without retaliation or disproportionate costs. **Immediate, unconditional recall is not imposed on every function**: in some contexts it might compromise oversight bodies' independence, procedural safeguards or service continuity. The conditions for withdrawal must themselves be justifiable and contestable. Distinguish **prospective withdrawal of a mandate**, challenge to a decision and retrospective invalidation of actions already undertaken.

[AP-0020 — Revocable delegated voting](../0020-revocable-delegated-democracy/en.md) remains a **specific option for research**, not a mechanism automatically adopted through AP-0006. Delegating a vote is only one form of delegation.

### Scope and relationships

Compare arrangements against the **two equally important aims** in [AP-0001](../0001-aims/en.md). Preventing arbitrary interference does not, on its own, provide the effective opportunities for self-determination examined by [AP-0002](../0002-emancipation/en.md). Conversely, gains in coordination cannot simply cancel out newly created domination without examination and contestable reasons. Assess affected people, alternative scenarios, minority impacts and unintended consequences.

These principles set no universal institutional design, number of bodies, mandate duration, voting rule or recall procedure. Specific implementation belongs in separate, assessable institutional proposals.

## Alternatives

Compare without a preassigned ranking:
- **Strongly constrained central authority:** potential continuity, clearer responsibility and coordination; risks of capture, distance and residual concentrated power.
- **Decentralization, federal arrangements or polycentric governance:** possibilities for local adaptation, parallel experimentation and mutual checks; coordination costs, territorial inequality and conflicts of jurisdiction.
- **Maximal fragmentation or “atomization”:** may reduce some formal monopolies but also multiply unaccountable local authorities, obstruction and hidden hierarchies.
- **Mutual oversight, independent bodies and remedies:** can separate authority from review; risks of collusion, watchdog capture and the recurring question of who checks those who check.
- **Participation and affected people's oversight:** may increase opportunities to challenge and revise rules; relies on access, minority safeguards and protection for dissenters.
- **Bounded delegation, fixed or revocable mandates, sortition and rotation:** different combinations of expertise, independence, responsibility and continuity. Instant recall is not necessarily appropriate to every function.

Compare **real institutional configurations**, not just labels. Specific forms of voting and representation are separately explored in [AP-0020](../0020-revocable-delegated-democracy/en.md).

## Criticism and limitations

- **Underdetermined criterion:** “controlled distribution” may remain too abstract to choose between rival institutional designs. Specify context, actors, competences, time horizons and trade-offs without pretending there is a universal metric.
- **Oversight regress:** appointing a new authority to oversee every overseer may proliferate bodies without producing meaningful checks; combine different safeguards with acknowledged weaknesses.
- **Capture and collusion:** formally distinct authorities may cooperate against those affected; formal separation does not establish actual independence.
- **Informal opacity:** funders, networks and experts can exert hard-to-observe influence; interventions against such influence can themselves create surveillance, exclusion or ideological control.
- **Delegation and urgency:** immediate withdrawal can harm continuity and independence, but an entrenched delegate can render appeals ineffective. Emergency decisions require justified limits and meaningful retrospective review.
- **Participation costs:** time, access to information, language and digital skills, material security and fear of retaliation can render formal oversight inaccessible.
- **Majorities and minorities:** majority control can still be arbitrary toward minorities; participation alone does not exclude this possibility.
- **Trade-offs with action:** reducing arbitrariness does not automatically expand opportunities to act; heavy oversight can impair urgent collective coordination. Evaluate the two aims separately.
- **Limits of transfer:** studies of common resources, government agencies or online voting do not establish general effectiveness for every political society.

## Evaluation

### Questions for inquiry, not adopted universal metrics

1. **Who actually decides?** Map formal competences, agenda-setting, information access, funds, expertise, influence networks and control of implementation.
2. **Who is affected?** Identify persons, groups, minorities and non-participants affected, including those without formal voting rights.
3. **Justification and limits:** are decisions and rules explainable to those affected? Which safeguards work in practice, and which arbitrary interventions remain credible?
4. **Oversight of decisions:** who can demand reasons, seek reconsideration, obtain an effective remedy and avoid retaliation?
5. **Oversight of rules:** who can propose revising, limiting or withdrawing an organ's authority? Can oversight bodies themselves be challenged?
6. **Delegation:** which competences are assigned, for how long, with what information and safeguards? Is recall or amendment practicable without unjustified harm to rights and continuity?
7. **Coordination and emancipation:** how do arrangements affect effective opportunities to act, information quality, decision speed, access to services and capacity to revise collective aims?
8. **Effects and corrections:** do new informal concentrations, conflicts, costs or domination arise? Are there credible mechanisms to identify and correct them?

### Comparative boundary cases

- **Strictly checked centralized authority** versus **widely autonomous but unaccountable local bodies**: distribution and domination do not necessarily move in tandem.
- **An assembly with formally equal votes** in which a narrow network sets the agenda and controls the information: investigate informal power without automatically assuming abuse.
- **An expert serving an independently supervised, bounded mandate** who is threatened with immediate removal for an unpopular finding: examine meaningful control versus necessary independence.
- **A mandate de facto impossible to revoke** because remedies are prohibitively costly versus **instant dismissal without procedure** damaging service continuity or individual protections.
- **A proliferation of oversight offices** creating blurred accountability or collusion: compare usable remedies and traceable responsibility.

Compare at least **retaining, modifying and replacing** the existing arrangement. Separate hypotheses, verified evidence and uncertainty; outcomes may remain indeterminate. These cases do not empirically prove a model.

## Proposed Manifesto Entry

**Distribution and oversight of power** are means of supporting emancipation and preventing domination, not ends in themselves. Their design must be assessed against the actual effects of both concentration and fragmentation on individuals and collectives.

All power, **including the authority of oversight bodies**, must be justifiable, limited and effectively controllable. Oversight applies both to decisions and to the rules that create authorities. It must examine **formal and informal power**, without treating every difference in influence, resources or expertise as domination.

Coordination and **delegation** can serve collective action when they remain justifiable, limited, contestable and revisable. **Recall** is one possible oversight mechanism, neither a sufficient safeguard nor a universal requirement for immediate and unconditional dismissal in every role. Distribution, delegation and oversight must preserve effective remedies and possibilities for institutional change without imposing a single institutional architecture.

## Research

### Comparative review — 17 November 2026

| Approach | Contribution to AP-0006 | Difference, limitation or caution |
|---|---|---|
| **Polycentric governance — Elinor Ostrom** [1] | Multiple decision centers may cooperate, adapt to context and coordinate across scales. | More centers or decentralization alone do not guarantee non-domination; assess coordination, access and accountability. |
| **Republican non-domination — Pettit and related debates** [2] | Credible exposure to arbitrary power matters even without observed abuse; properly controlled discretion is not necessarily domination. | AP-0003 establishes **its own** complementary justification, limitation and oversight tests, not a wholesale adoption of republican theory. |
| **Principal–agent theory — Sean Gailmard** [3] | Delegation, information asymmetry, agent responsiveness and institutional accountability can be examined separately. | A family of models, not a complete account of human motives or a normative proof of non-domination. |
| **Parliamentary delegation — Kaare Strøm** [4] | Chains of delegation illuminate problems of transparency and accountability. | Research concerns parliamentary systems and does not establish any universally superior delegation type. |
| **Jo Freeman — “Tyranny of Structurelessness”** [5] | Informally structured groups may develop less visible elites and hierarchies. | Historical argumentative essay, not empirical proof that horizontal organization is necessarily dominating. |
| **Liquid democracy — Campbell and colleagues** [6] | Two experiments suggest that excessive vote delegation may diminish independent informational diversity and worsen outcomes in studied tasks. | Context-specific findings, not a refutation of all political delegation; voting differs from administrative mandates. |
| **Liquid democracy in online organizations — Hall and Miyazaki** [7] | An analysis of DAOs reports clustered delegations and limited participation, alongside some potential bottom-up gains. | Token-based voting in blockchain projects **does not directly generalize** to one-person-one-vote citizenship systems. |

### Analytical synthesis

These sources do not determine an optimal institutional architecture. They help distinguish **allocation/distribution, exercise, oversight of decisions and oversight of the rules**, while explicitly investigating informal authority. **Distribution is instrumental; overseers must be controllable; delegation can be useful; recall safeguards depend on context.**

Evaluate consequences **jointly but separately** for emancipation (AP-0002) and non-domination (AP-0003), under AP-0001. Neither institutional compliance with these normative principles nor formal safeguards guarantees results in practice.

### Orientations explicitly accepted by the initiator — 17 November 2026

In the discussions preceding this revision, the initiator accepted: **(1)** distribution as a means rather than an end; **(2)** control over overseers and over the rules establishing power as well as its exercise; **(3)** examination of both formal and informal power without equating influence and domination; **(4)** justifiable, limited and effectively controlled delegation, with recall an optional mechanism but no universal requirement of instant, unconditional withdrawal. **Acceptance of these conceptual orientations does not constitute formal editorial approval of AP-0006.**

### Further research and unresolved questions

Specify procedures for access to information, challenge, appeals and institutional correction in subsequent proposals; investigate expertise and watchdog independence, privacy versus transparency, and resilience under emergencies. Comparative testing is required before claiming any specific arrangement works generally. [AP-0020 — Revocable delegated voting](../0020-revocable-delegated-democracy/en.md) remains a separate proposal.

The [economic-political notes](https://github.com/Nirgalz/Anarchopeia/blob/0d8963af515be54957f767531017aff3d6881955/txt/ecopolitique.txt) propose **one person, one vote**, citizen initiatives and issue-by-issue, revocable delegation to experts or representatives.
